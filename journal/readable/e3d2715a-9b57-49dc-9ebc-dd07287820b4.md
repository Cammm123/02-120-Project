**Claude:** ## 1) How many cells

Yes, we found three sources on Oct 7. They're in `research/cell-rules-candidates.md`:

| Source | Cells | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Luebeck & Moolgavkar 2002 | about 10^8 colon stem cells | confirmed |

All of these are about the colon. We don't have a lung number yet.

We can't simulate 10^8 cells per person, but in v1 we don't need to. When hits are rare, a person's risk by age t is about N × C(t, r) × p^r. That means N and p trade off against each other:

- **N (number of cells)** only raises or lowers the whole line. It doesn't change the slope.
- **p (chance of a hit per year)** changes the height and also controls the bend.

So 100 cells with p = 0.005 behaves almost like 10^8 cells with p = 0.005 × (100 / 10^8)^(1/4) ≈ 0.00016. We're shrinking the person for speed, and the shape of the curve stays the same.

The paper numbers start to matter in later versions. When cells sit in crypts and divide, Nicholson's 7 stem cells per crypt could set the grid layout.

## 2) Why 20,000 people

There was no real logic. I picked 20,000 as a round number that gives about 1,750 cases and should run fast. Here's a way to choose it properly.

A random count is uncertain by about √(count). For example, if 100 cases are expected, you'll usually get 90–110 (±10%).

The fewest cases come in the youngest group we fit, ages 25–29:

| People | Expected cases at 25–29 | Noise |
|---|---|---|
| 20,000 | about 16 | √16 = 4, so ±25% |
| about 123,000 | 100 | ±10% |

**Rule:** pick the smallest number of people that gives at least 100 expected cases in the 25–29 group. The catch is speed: 123,000 people is about 6× the work. We'll time it when we build `simulatePopulation`. Keeping 20,000 for now is fine.

## 3) Why r = 4 and p = 0.005

**r = 4:** I picked it for the math check, not for biology. Slope 3 is easy to recognize, and a small r runs fast. There are real-data choices for later:
- Armitage & Doll's Table I slopes are 5–6.5, so r = 6–7.
- CI5 England lung cancer in men has a slope of about 8, so r ≈ 9.

**p = 0.005:** this one has some logic, as a trade-off.
- A smaller p gives a slope closer to r − 1 but fewer cases. At p = 0.002 the slope is 2.99, but there are only about 56 cases.
- A larger p gives more cases but more bending. At p = 0.005 there are about 1,750 cases, with a slope of 2.84.

The bend grows with p × age, so a rule could be "the largest p that keeps the exact slope within 0.2 of r − 1." Whatever we pick, the exact formula tells us the right slope.

## 4) The rate formula

The rate means **cases per year spent at risk**. The question is how to count those years. Take the age 60–64 group with 1,000 cancer-free people at age 60, where 50 get cancer during the group:

- **Simple:** assume everyone was at risk all 5 years. That's 1,000 × 5 = 5,000 person-years, so the rate is 50 / 5,000 = 0.0100.
- **Exact person-years:** someone who got cancer at 61 was only at risk for 2 of those years (60 and 61). If the 50 cases average 3 years each, that's 950 × 5 + 50 × 3 = 4,900 person-years, so the rate is 50 / 4,900 = 0.0102.

When cancer is rare, the two barely differ. When it's common, they do. In hand check 3 (one hit, p = 0.1):
- simple gives 0.0819;
- exact person-years gives exactly 0.1000, which is p itself.

The exact count is cleaner and closer to how CI5 counts, so I'd lean that way. Hand check 3 would then become "rate = p".

## 5) The tolerance

The simulation is random, so it never matches the formula exactly. The tolerance is how far off counts as "still correct" before the test fails.

- **3 standard deviations:** if 100 cases are expected, the standard deviation is about √100 = 10, so 70–130 passes. A correct program lands inside this range 99.7% of the time. ### On realism

- a real per-cell mutation rate of about 1.4×10⁻⁶ per year (their Table 1, for the APC gene);

## 3) What p is

- **In biology,** p is the chance per year that one cell gets the specific mutation for its next hit. It's roughly a mutation rate per cell per year, and real values are around 10⁻⁶.

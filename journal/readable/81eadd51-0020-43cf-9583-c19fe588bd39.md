**Claude:** ## What `logLogFit` does

**The idea:** if a rate grows like a power of age, `rate = c × age^k`, then taking the log of both sides turns that curve into a straight line:

```
log(rate) = log(c) + k × log(age)
   y      = intercept + slope × x
```

The power k becomes the slope. So `logLogFit` takes logs of the ages and rates, then hands them to the `fitLine` you already built and tested. It adds nothing new except the logs and skipping groups that have 0 cases, since log(0) doesn't exist.

**Numbers you can check** (rate = age³, logs base 10):

| age | rate | log(age) | log(rate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1,000 | 1.000 | 3.000 |
| 20 | 8,000 | 1.301 | 3.903 |

Slope = (3.903 − 3.000) / (1.301 − 1.000) = 0.903 / 0.301 = **3**. In plain words: doubling the age multiplies the rate by 2³ = 8.

```
 rate vs age (curves up)          log(rate) vs log(age) (straight)
 |                    *           |                  *
 |                 *              |              *
 |             *                  |          *
 |       *                        |      *
 |*  *                            |  *
 +-------------------- age        +-------------------- log(age)
```

## Power vs exponential

- **Power** (`rate = age³`): the rate multiplies by the same amount each time age *doubles*.
- **Exponential** (e.g. the rate doubles every 10 years): the rate multiplies by the same amount for every *fixed number of years added*.

Here's how much each one grows over 10 years, at a young age and an old age:

| 10-year step | age³ grows by | "doubles every 10 years" grows by |
|---|---|---|
| 20 → 30 | (30/20)³ = **3.4×** | **2×** |
| 70 → 80 | (80/70)³ = **1.5×** | **2×** |

Under the power law, the yearly percent growth slows down with age. Under the exponential, it stays the same. Exponential growth actually overtakes any power eventually, so the model is not "super exponential". It is the slower of the two.

Each kind turns straight on a different graph:
- power → straight on **log(rate) vs log(age)**: `logLogFit`, whose slope ≈ r − 1 hits
- exponential → straight on **log(rate) vs age**

So the log-log slope isn't just a readability trick. It's the measurement itself: it tells you how many hits a cell needs. A plain-axis graph can't give you that number.

## Idea 2: from one cell to a whole person

You found that one cell (r = 2, p = 0.5) is still below 2 hits after 2 years with chance **3/4**. A person has **N** cells, and they're cancer-free only if **every** cell is still below r.

Each cell flips its own coins, so what one cell does doesn't change the others. When events are independent like that, "all of them happen" means you **multiply** the chances:

```
S(t) = (chance one cell is still below r) ^ N
```

**Example with numbers:** N = 2 cells, r = 2, p = 0.5, t = 2 years.

```
          cell 1 OK (3/4)   cell 1 done (1/4)
cell 2 OK (3/4)     9/16  ✓        3/16
cell 2 done (1/4)   3/16           1/16
```

Only the top-left box is cancer-free: **S(2) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16**. The other three boxes add up to 7/16, the chance that at least one cell reached 2 hits.

This is the formula already in `spec.txt`, written as:
```
S(t) = (1 - P(one cell done by t))^N
```
Here P(one cell done) = 1/4, so 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, and (3/4)² = 9/16.

**Why this matters for the project:** with N = 100 cells, even a small chance per cell adds up fast. That's why the default p is as small as 0.005. Later this is also where the computer-arithmetic trap in the spec comes in: (1 − tiny)^N can round to exactly 1.

## Idea 3: what `expectedRates` does

```
           S(g) - S(g+5)              <- share of people who get cancer in the group
```

## Idea 4: the computer-arithmetic trap

A computer keeps only about 16 digits. Your real chances are far smaller than that, so some obvious ways of computing them give **exactly 0**. I just ran this:

```
1 - 1e-17          = 1
1 - (1 - 1e-17)    = 0    (should be 1e-17)
(1 - 1e-27) ** 1e8 = 1    (should be just under 1)
1 - that           = 0    (should be 1e-19)
-expm1(1e8 * log1p(-1e-27)) = 1.0000000000000001e-19   <- right
```

There are two traps, and the spec has a rule for each:

1. **"One cell done" = 1 − P(fewer than r hits).** P(fewer than r) is something like 0.9999999…, which rounds to 1, so the answer comes out as 0. **Rule: add up P(exactly r) + P(exactly r+1) + … directly.** Those terms are tiny, but adding tiny numbers keeps all their digits.
2. **S = (1 − q)^N with tiny q.** 1 − q rounds to 1, so S = 1, meaning no risk at all. **Rule: use `Math.log1p` and `Math.expm1`**, which compute log(1 + x) and eˣ − 1 without ever forming the rounded "1 + x". In the last line above, that gives the right answer.

**Does it matter for your default settings?** Barely. With p = 0.005 the numbers stay well inside 16 digits. It matters for realistic settings: the spec's r = 6 case has a chance of about 3.3e-19. The tests will include a case like that, so a version that only works for your defaults can't pass.

## Idea 5: one Binomial term without huge numbers

`cellDoneChance` adds up terms of the form

```
P(exactly j hits in t years) = C(t, j) × p^j × (1 − p)^(t − j)
```

C(t, j) counts the orderings, for example which j of the t years were hits. The textbook formula t! / (j! (t − j)!) needs 85!, which is about 10¹²⁸: absurdly large, and it loses digits when you divide. Instead, build C(t, j) as a product of j small fractions:

```
C(t, j) = (t / 1) × ((t − 1) / 2) × ((t − 2) / 3) × ... × ((t − j + 1) / j)
```

**Example:** C(4, 2) = (4/1) × (3/2) = 6. Check by listing which 2 of 4 years are hits: 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34, so 6 ways. ✓

Every step stays a reasonable size, and the largest result for your ages is C(85, 42) ≈ 10²⁴, which a double holds easily.

The other two parts are fine as plain powers. p^j for a tiny p, like (10⁻⁶)⁷ = 10⁻⁴², is nowhere near the smallest number a double can hold (about 10⁻³⁰⁸). In JavaScript, `0 ** 0` is 1, so p = 0 and p = 1 work without special cases.

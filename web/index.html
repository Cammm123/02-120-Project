How many hits make a cancer?

In this model, each person has N cells, and a cell turns into cancer after r hits (mutations), each with chance p per year (Armitage & Doll, 1954). Plot the cancer rate against age with both axes on log scales (left graph): the line's slope tells you how many hits are needed, about r − 1. Compare it with real cancers to estimate their r.

How to read these

Gap for one age group (z): how many "noise units" the group is off.

z=simulated cancers−expected cancersexpected cancers

Random chance alone usually keeps z between −2 and +2. The page shows its size, ignoring whether it's too many or too few. Colors for the largest gap: up to 2 plain; 2 to 3 yellow; over 3 red. With 12 age groups, one above 2 happens in about half of correct runs, so yellow on its own is not a problem.

Total miss: every group's gap squared, added up (k = number of age groups judged).

total miss=∑i=1kzi2

With pure chance it averages k (about 12). Pass line, set by running 20 seeds:

pass line=k+32k(26.7 when k = 12)

R² on the slope tiles: how straight the points are around their own line on the log-log graph (1 = perfectly straight).

R2=1−∑(miss from the line)2∑(distance from the average)2

It is about straightness, not agreement: real lung cancer has R² 0.990 but a much steeper slope than the model at r = 4. Compare slopes to compare lines, and use the total miss to check the simulation.

Where the real numbers come from

Source: Cancer Incidence in Five Continents, Volume XII (CI5-XII), from the International Agency for Research on Cancer. It counts new cancer cases (not deaths) and the population at risk, by five-year age group. We use one registry: England, 2013–2017, men, and three cancers: lung, stomach and colon.

Rate = new cases ÷ person-years (the number of years people of that age were alive in the registry, added up). For example, colon cancer at ages 60–64: 6,082 cases ÷ 7,188,268 person-years = 84.6 per 100,000.

Slope: fitted on the log-log graph over ages 25–84, the same ages as the model. Younger ages are mostly childhood cancers, which work differently (for example, Knudson's two-hit retinoblastoma), and 85+ is one open-ended group with no middle age.

Why the real dots are moved up or down: the model's N and p are scaled-up stand-ins, so its height isn't meant to match real rates; only the shape is. The real rates are multiplied by one number so the real line meets the model's exact line at age 62.5. That moves the line without changing its slope.
